THE LIGHTHOUSE KEEPER'S APPRENTICE
===============================

--- Page 1 ---

The island was exactly one mile across at its widest point, and Tomas knew every single foot of it. He had arrived twelve years ago as a boy of nine, sent to live with his uncle Aldric after the fever took his parents, and in all that time he had never once set foot on the mainland. The island had become his whole world: the rocky southern shore where the seals basked in summer, the small sheltered cove where the supply boat anchored twice a month, the wind-bent pines that clung to the eastern ridge, and at the northern tip, tall and white and eternal, the lighthouse.

His uncle kept the light, as his uncle's father had before him, and as his father before that. The family had tended the flame for six generations, and Aldric often said that the sea had claims on their blood. Tomas was never sure whether this was meant as a warning or a promise.

Each evening, an hour before dusk, they climbed the hundred and twelve steps of the spiral staircase together. Tomas would carry the oil, and his uncle would carry the tools, and they would perform the same ritual they had performed a thousand times: check the wicks, clean the glass, wind the great clockwork mechanism that rotated the lens. The lens itself was a masterpiece of brass and crystal, older than any living person, and when the sun caught it at the right angle it threw rainbows across the tower walls like scattered jewels.

"Light it," Aldric would say, and Tomas would strike the match, and for one suspended moment the flame would hover between the matchstick and the wick — a moment Tomas came to think of as the hinge of the day, the point where the island handed itself over to the dark.

The work was lonely, but Tomas rarely felt lonely. The island was full of voices if you knew how to listen: the arguing of gulls, the low boom of the waves against the cliffs, the creak and sigh of the tower in high winds. His uncle taught him to read the sky the way other men read books — a red dawn meant rain by noon, a ring around the moon meant trouble within three days. He taught him the names of every passing ship by silhouette alone, and how to judge distance by the way sound carried over water.

But of all his uncle's lessons, the one Tomas remembered best came on a night of terrible storm, when the waves struck the tower hard enough to rattle the tools on their hooks. Tomas, then thirteen, was frightened, and said so. His uncle did not laugh at him or dismiss his fear. He simply put a hand on his shoulder and pointed out the window, to where the beam swept through the sheets of rain, out and back, out and back, patient as a heartbeat.

"Somewhere out there," Aldric said, "there's a man on a ship who cannot see his own hand in front of his face. He is more frightened than you are. And the only thing standing between him and the rocks is that light. Remember, when you're afraid: fear is just the sound of the world asking you to do your job anyway."

Tomas remembered. He would remember it for the rest of his life.

The letter arrived on the supply boat in the spring of his twenty-first year, and it changed everything. The mainland authorities had decided to automate the lighthouse. A new electric lamp, they wrote, would be installed by autumn. The keeper's services would no longer be required, though a small pension would be provided, and the family was welcome to remain in the cottage until the end of the year.

His uncle read the letter twice, then folded it carefully and set it on the table. He said nothing for a long time. That evening they climbed the hundred and twelve steps as always, lit the lamp as always, and watched the beam sweep out over a calm and pewter-colored sea. It was Tomas who finally broke the silence.

"Six generations," he said. "And it ends with a machine."

His uncle was quiet for a while. "Machines end nothing," he said at last. "A light is a light. The sailors out there don't care whether the flame comes from oil or electricity. They care that it burns, and that it's true. The lamp was never the important part, Tomas."

"Then what is?"

His uncle tapped his own chest, above the heart. "The keeping. Someone who decides, every night, that the light will not fail. A machine can hold a flame. It cannot keep a promise. That part was always ours — and nobody can automate it away, because it was never theirs to take."

They stayed through the autumn. Tomas helped the engineers install the new apparatus, and he learned its mechanisms carefully, thoroughly, because his uncle insisted that if the family's duty was to end, it would end properly — with the handover done well, the tower left perfect, the glass polished one final time. On the last night, they climbed the steps together for the thousandth time and stood watching the old lamp burn its last hours.

At dawn, they extinguished the flame by hand, together, and the six-generation vigil came to its close.

Tomas left the island that winter, but he did not leave the keeping behind. He found work on the mainland with the coastal service, tending the new lights up and down the coast, teaching young apprentices the names of ships and the language of the sky. And on every stormy night, when the rain hammered the windows and the wind howled like something alive, he would stand at whatever tower he was given and watch the beam sweep out over the black water, out and back, out and back, patient as a heartbeat.

Somewhere out there, he knew, someone was afraid. And the light was burning anyway.

Fear is just the sound of the world asking you to do your job.

He kept the promise. He kept it all his life.
